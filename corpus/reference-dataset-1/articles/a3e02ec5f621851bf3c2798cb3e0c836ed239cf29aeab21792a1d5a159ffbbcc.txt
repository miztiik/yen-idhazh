This is a Build Canada Better briefing by Adamant, one of a five-part series documenting nation-building projects that will make Canada work better and secure our economic future for generations.
Canada has some of the world’s best wind, solar, and hydropower resources, and huge geothermal potential. The problem is these resources are spread across provinces that barely trade electricity with each other. Today, provinces trade more power with the United States than they do with one another.
Wiring Canada together with new east-west transmission lines would change that. It would cut electricity costs for homes and businesses, create tens of thousands of well-paid jobs from coast to coast, attract investment in clean industry, and make Canada less reliant on American markets to buy or use Canadian power. The Canadian Climate Institute calls it Canada’s top nation-building project.
The federal government’s National Electricity Strategy, announced in May, lays out an intention to connect Canada’s fragmented grids. That is good news and it needs to be done right: not as a backdoor way for taxpayers to subsidize electricity to remote LNG plants, but as a priority project using domestic steel and aluminum that will deliver clean power to Canadian families from coast to coast.
A national program building new East-West transmission lines will:
• Reduce electricity costs for homes and businesses in a high-demand, electrified future
• Create tens of thousands of well-paying jobs in all areas of the country
• Attract clean industry investment and business opportunities
• Advance Indigenous economic reconciliation
• Make Canada more energy secure and resilient in the face of global economic pressures
Why Canada’s Grids Need to be Connected
Canada’s electricity systems were built province by province, each designed to serve its own population with little thought for fellow Canadians across provincial boundaries. The result is a patchwork of isolated islands that barely talk to each other. Where connections do exist, they are largely oriented toward trade with American states, not with Canadian neighbours.
More than 80% of Canada’s electricity trade flows south into the U.S., not east-west between provinces, a 2025 Dunsky Energy + Climate webinar explained. Canada operates 37 cross-border transmission lines with American utilities, but has almost no equivalent capacity connecting provinces to each other, noted a 2025 call from more than 100 organizations urging the federal government “to prioritize building out Canada’s east-west electricity grid with renewable energy, while upholding workers’ and Indigenous rights.”
Interties between provinces will enable instantaneous trade, making Canadian electricity systems more resilient not just to geopolitical shocks but also to droughts, cold snaps, heat waves, and outages, writes the Canadian Climate Institute. An east-west grid will lower costs, create jobs, and make Canada harder to push around.
No Province is an Island
The opportunity this project will create is enormous, but to act on it we need to be honest about what’s actually going on in our fractured system. British Columbia’s large hydroelectric system and Alberta’s abundant wind and solar potential make them natural partners, but the transmission link between them is sorely underutilized. Quebec and Newfoundland have world-class hydro resources, meanwhile, and provinces such as Ontario and Nova Scotia have growing clean electricity needs that are not yet being met.
Interprovincial alliances are a faster way to get where we want to go. Rather than each province building out enough generation to meet its own needs, we should be sharing power with each other, with better infrastructure and simplified markets.
This is what the U.S. already does, with several regional transmission organizations that share electricity across state lines. The Midcontinent Independent System Operator (MISO), for example, connects 15 states and stretches 2,000 kilometres from Michigan to Louisiana. Ironically, Manitoba is connected into the MISO interchange—more integrated with the U.S. than with its Canadian neighbours.
A national, east-west grid will mean everyone has something to offer and everyone has something to gain. It will be a profound shift towards Canadian independence and resilience—focusing less on exporting power to American consumers and more on strengthening our own communities by sharing it here.
Electricity Demand is Starting to Surge
Canada’s electricity system is heading into a period of demand growth unlike anything it has seen before, the Pembina Institute reports. Electric vehicles, heat pumps, industrial electrification, and data centres are all converging on the grid at the same time. The Public Policy Forum notes [pdf] that the output of Canada’s electricity grid output will need to be 2.2 to 3.4 times larger by 2050 in order to meet demand.
The demand surge is already happening, and the question is whether we will first create the transmission infrastructure and market mechanisms to let power flow where it is needed—or leave our best clean energy resources locked in provincial silos while our neighbours pay inflated rates for scarce power.
Don’t Lock Us In
We have the goods to triple our clean power production to meet demand without furthering climate and air pollution with fossil fuels. The Prairies have effectively unlimited wind and solar potential, notes the Canadian Climate Institute. Atlantic Canada has massive offshore wind resources. B.C., Manitoba, Quebec, and Newfoundland and Labrador have established reservoir hydro that can act as a “battery” to balance intermittent renewables elsewhere. But these renewable energy assets—current and future—will be cut off from their potential if there is no way to move the power to where it is needed, dimming the economic value they could generate.
The Economic Case
• A competitive edge for attracting clean industry: Manufacturers, data centres, and industrial operators are increasingly choosing locations based on access to clean, affordable, and reliable electricity. Without sufficient transmission capacity, Canada cannot offer the scale of clean power that major industries require. An interconnected grid makes Canada a more competitive destination for the businesses that will define the next generation of the economy. The Canadian Climate Institute specifically notes that grid integration would attract investment and grow the Canadian economy.
• Real jobs in construction, trades, and operations: Building and running transmission infrastructure creates jobs across the country, often in remote and rural areas. The Ontario East-West Tie, a 450-kilometre line through Northwestern Ontario, required approximately 2,600 person-years of labour during construction, writes NextEra Energy. This project alone generated more than $200 million in economic benefits—and Indigenous workers made up more than half the peak construction workforce. Many went on to permanent careers in electrical engineering, power systems management, and grid operations with utilities and energy companies after the project was complete.
The skills built on a transmission line do not disappear when construction ends. They become the workforce that runs the grid.
The Canadian Building Trades Union estimated in its 2025 report Jobs for Today that building out an additional 50,000 kilometres of electrical transmission in Canada—expanding the current grid by 30%—would result in between 155,000 and 233,000 person-years of construction labour, a huge opportunity for those in the skilled trades.
Nationally, Canada’s electricity sector already employs more than 110,000 people. Between 2023 and 2028, the sector expects 28,000 job openings, according to Natural Resources Canada, with another 130,000 projected between 2028 and 2050. These are skilled, well-paid, often unionized positions. The International Brotherhood of Electrical Workers has stated that its members across Canada are ready to build a national electrical network, creating well-paid union jobs in the communities where those workers live.
• Return on federal investment: The financial case for federal investment is strong. As the C.D. Howe Institute has highlighted, energy system modelling found that doubling B.C.-Alberta interconnection capacity would return nearly $6 for every dollar spent, netting $1.7 billion in economic benefits by 2050. Adding capacity between Manitoba and Saskatchewan would return $4.50 for every dollar spent, with $2.3 billion in net benefits over the same period.
• Lower electricity bills in a high-demand future: Electricity demand is going up. Connecting provincial grids gives utilities access to a wider pool of power, meaning they don’t have to build as much redundant capacity in each province. This helps keep rates lower than they would otherwise be as demand grows. A more interconnected grid also makes it easier to absorb wind and solar power, which are now the cheapest sources of new electricity generation in Canada, notes the Canadian Climate Institute.
• Economic opportunity for Indigenous communities: Every new transmission line built to wire Canada together will cross through many Indigenous territories. That is an opportunity to build something better. The Indigenous Power Coalition (IPC), launched in March 2026, argues that Indigenous leadership is not just an obligation under Canadian law, it is the smartest path to actually getting these projects built. Nations can work across provincial borders in ways that provincial governments often cannot, advancing collaborative electricity planning across regions with political and geographic relationships that long predate Confederation.
The IPC report “It Is Our Time” identifies $5.2 billion in potential Indigenous equity participation in transmission infrastructure. The Wataynikaneyap transmission line in northern Ontario, majority-owned by 24 First Nations, demonstrates what this looks like in practice. Hydro One has committed to 50% Indigenous equity ownership in new Ontario transmission lines.
Shifting Indigenous Nations from consulted rights-holders to project proponents and leaders advances reconciliation while unlocking projects that have been stuck for years, generating stable long-term revenue for communities and building the workforce Canada needs to run the grid it is building—all without adding to climate pollution.
A March 2026 Leger poll found that 76% of Canadians support upgrading Canada’s electricity grid to reduce economic reliance on energy exports to the United States. Support is consistent across every region. More than 100 organizations across Canada—including Indigenous rights groups, labour unions, and clean energy and environmental advocates— have recommended a suite of actions by the federal government, following a review of Canada’s National Clean Electricity Strategy.
These are the key recommendations of their review:
• On transmission infrastructure: Make connecting Canada’s electricity systems a federal priority, starting with the most strategic interprovincial and inter-regional links. De-risk these projects with a dedicated federal financial package of $20 billion over five years, including an investment tax credit for Canadian steel and aluminum and continued Canada Investment Bank loan support. The federal government should also be at the table—convening, planning, and where appropriate, holding an ownership stake through the Canada Development Investment Corporation alongside provincial and Indigenous partners.
• On Indigenous participation: Put the United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples into practice. That means upholding Indigenous rights, including free, prior and informed consent—not as a process obligation, but as the foundation these projects are built on. Back Indigenous-owned renewable energy and grid infrastructure with $5 billion in federal investment over five years, and keep the Indigenous loan guarantee program running and well-funded.
• On workers: Tie training and prevailing wage standards to the Clean Economy investment tax credits, and require Canadian content for aluminum, steel, and other key manufacturing inputs. Workers in emissions-intensive generation need more than good intentions — they need employment guarantees, skills training, income protection, and transition support that’s actually there when they need it.
• On clean electricity regulation: Canada has committed to a net-zero electricity grid, and the federal Clean Electricity Regulations are how that commitment becomes real. Implement them without provincial carve-outs or equivalency agreements that would hollow them out.
The Bottom Line
Canada has world-class clean energy resources, but we lack the infrastructure to share them effectively. East-west transmission will wire Canada together, better preparing us for the electrified future economy. The federal funds required are significant, but this is an investment that will pay off many times over, not least by helping ensure the resilience of the grid. Every year without action is another year of stranded renewables, more climate pollution, higher power bills, and lost opportunity.
