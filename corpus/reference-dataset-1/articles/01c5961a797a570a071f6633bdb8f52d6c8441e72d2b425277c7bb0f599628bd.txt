What are the strengths of open source AI? How do you compare different AI models? And how is access to the frontier bought?
This is a primer for a series of blogs on the uncertain futures of open source AI (OSAI) and its implications for low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). If you already feel confident in your knowledge of OSAI, skip to the next part.
General-purpose AI models, such as large-language models, are composed of a large number of weights (parameters) which represent the association between words and concepts. As the model is trained, these weights are adjusted to reflect these associations (known as “pre-training”) as well as abide by preferences set for the model’s responses (largely sitting in the bucket of “post-training”). Open up the black box of general-purpose AI, and you’ll see inside that it’s composed of these weights. The weights, in this sense, are the model. If you want the model, you need the weights. If you want to change the model, you need access to adjust the weights.
Focusing on the weights also helps us gain a clearer picture on what makes a model open source. If you can access the model’s weights, then you can conduct significant auditing and modify them further through new training, so we can call the model open source (for the purposes of this series, at least). Put plainly, if a member of the public can download the weights from a popular repository, such as Hugging Face, then the model is open source.[1]
Open source AI models are often touted as an important solution for LMICs, but these arguments typically don’t go beyond surface level positive associations. Openness has a positive normative association, but supposed transparency doesn’t yield utility unless it can be linked to (i) specific needs from users and developers, and (ii) the utilities are earned by deploying sufficient capital and capacity (often requiring talent or input from the state).
Why Open Source AI?
So what actually are some of the strengths of OSAI?
Political utility
Accessing the weights allows for a deeper level of auditing and transparency, but this requires sufficiently capable technical teams that can conduct the audits. A broad spectrum of audits and evaluations can be run by independent third-parties, but so far for open source models these have significantly lagged behind frontier models. For instance, Kimi K2.5 was not released with a safety evaluation and so an independent team based in the United States took it on themselves to run one.[2]
Being able to host the weights on local servers allows for controlling the data flow, ensuring that it does not leave your territory. This can be helpful for ensuring privacy and security in a range of critical functions, such as citizens’ tax records, but it requires hosting sufficiently large and secure data storages. The average LMIC may find a more secure option in models and buckets hosted on Google Cloud Platform than their own servers, but this would provide Google with some leverage.
Product utility
Downloading and adjusting the model weights enables a wide range of adaptations which we have argued elsewhere are key for unlocking economic and development impact from general-purpose AI.[3] That variations of OSAI are particularly suited to adaptations is probably one of its greatest strengths, but this adaptability is not a monopoly. Take the GPT range of models from OpenAI, which enables post-training (specialisation, as per our taxonomy here) through fine-tuning and offers a suite of smaller models which could be run using less inference. Access to the weights is still not offered in these API-based instances, but perhaps this is not required for tasks that are often done using an internet connection and without data privacy concerns.
OSAI is very capable at a broad range of tasks for competitive prices if hosted externally. Moreover, these capabilities are not only cost-effective but have also closely tracked the frontier. While this present parity may motivate investment for the future, we argue that positive benchmarks belie deeper vulnerabilities. For this, we need to turn to a discussion on evaluations and benchmarks.
Benchmarks and evaluations
General-purpose AI models are compared against one another using benchmarks: standardised assessments, ideally unseen by the model during training, which generate performance scores. Different 'benches' measure different capabilities, and a short history of what they’ve found can be summarised as measuring models’ better, broader, and faster capability attainment. They began with language tasks (e.g. GLUE, which tested of grammar, sentiment, and paraphrasing, and was saturated within a year of release), the graduated to reasoning (such as MMLU's exams across 57 academic subjects, and escalating to GPQA's deliberately "Google-proof" graduate-level science questions), and now increasingly measure real-world work (like OSWorld, which drops an agent into a real computer desktop and scores whether it can complete tasks like filling out a spreadsheet).
Benchmarks underlie current debates about the horse race between open- and closed-source AI. A gap in performance can be quoted as a score differential, or converted into a distance: how many months ago the frontier could do what the best open model can do today. This framing offers a sense of elegance, because it implies the follower is running the same course, and will eventually pass the same posts.
The gap reads very differently depending on where you look (this snapshot is from August 2026):[4]
- 4 to 7 months on cyber capabilities, down from 6 to 10 months in 2025 (UK AISI, July 2026)
- 0.2 months on a benchmark for US tax-and-benefit estimation, down from 4 months in April 2026 (PolicyEngine's PolicyBench, June 2026)[5]
- Around 4 months across a broad range of capabilities, up from 3 months between 2023 and 2025 (Epoch AI, May 2026)
For the big picture, it's a good idea to use a composite measure such as Epoch's Capabilities Index, rather than any single metric: individual benchmarks are in flux and paint an incomplete picture, as the difference in magnitude between cyber and policy analysis demonstrates.
Another point to keep in mind: benchmarks are not only a measuring stick but also a training target. Specify a reward for scoring well, run reinforcement learning against it, and a model will learn whichever route reaches the reward, whether or not that route generalises into the capability the benchmark claims to measure. In these cases, the measure and the curriculum are interchangeable, making some benchmarks clear examples of Goodhart’s law where teaching to the test has dominated capability attainment. That is one of the mechanisms which makes benchmarks an unreliable, if elegant, framing for this horserace.
How frontier access is bought
Given the distinction we've drawn between open models, closed models, and the frontier, how is access to that frontier acquired by governments? The common case for OSAI in LMICs promises high capabilities without compromises on sovereignty. But when governments need a capability which they cannot produce at home, they have often paid with slices of their own autonomy.
In May 2025, the United Arab Emirates agreed to build a data center campus in Abu Dhabi with up to half a million of Nvidia's most advanced chips each year. Despite G42, an Emirati company, building and owning the data centers, the UAE would not operate them; OpenAI and Oracle would. Before this deal, G42 had agreed to strip Huawei equipment out of its networks and sell off its Chinese holdings. The government brought the UAE's rules on sensitive technologies in line with American export controls. American regulations restrict what Emirati companies can do, in exchange for access to the best models available. These were the terms offered to one of the richest countries (per capita) on earth, and the implications for less affluent nations are more dire.
Nobody offers a technology-for-autonomy treaty to most LMICs. That bargain is implicit in commercial deals instead: we see Azure-only "sovereign" regions, an Nvidia-only "AI factory," free tokens that are cheap to accept but become the default and cause vendor lock-in. Sovereignty is rarely signed away through a single technology, but the accumulation of technology often has negative implications for it. This is the backdrop against which the promise of open models should be judged. We address that promise in Part 1.
