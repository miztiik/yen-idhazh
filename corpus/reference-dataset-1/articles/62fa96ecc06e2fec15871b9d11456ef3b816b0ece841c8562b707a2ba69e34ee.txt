The Missing Part
On 24 August the national working committee of India's Cockroach Janta Party met and concluded that it had been cheated.
On 25 July the movement had ended a sit-in at Jantar Mantar in central Delhi after the government agreed to three things — the maximum possible compensation for the families of students who had killed themselves after a leaked medical entrance paper, the withdrawal of police cases against protesters filed by the Centre and by states run by the ruling coalition, and formal talks on the group's five-point charter for examination reform.
None of it, the party says, has happened. No delegation has been in touch about the money. The government has not produced the list of first information reports (FIRs) it undertook to have quashed, and, according to the party, has declined to commit to producing one even before the Supreme Court. In a statement the group used the Hindi word vishwasghaat, a betrayal of trust, and warned against any attempt to "dilute, delay, reinterpret or dishonour" what had been agreed.
So it is going back. On 5 September, which is Teachers' Day in India, the party will march from India Gate to the New Delhi police headquarters, with the families of the dead students and of those it says were injured by police at the front. "We have been forced to return to the streets," it said. "A government cannot take the trust of an entire generation, use it to send young people home, and then quietly retreat from its own word."
A satirical Instagram account with a cartoon insect for a logo acquired more than 20 million followers in a fortnight, put students on the streets of six cities, defeated the Indian government in the Delhi High Court and, by the end of July, had extracted three commitments from it. What it never acquired was any means of making those commitments stick.
Both halves of that story, the speed of the victory and the emptiness of it, are described in a manual published in 2015 by a Serbian activist who helped bring down Slobodan Milosevic. The Cockroach Janta Party has almost certainly never read it. It has been running about half of it anyway, and the half it skipped is the half that makes a win enforceable.
A Brand, Not A Leader
Srdja Popovic was 25 when he became one of 11 students who founded Otpor!, the movement that helped force Milosevic from power in October 2000. He has spent the quarter-century since exporting the method, latterly through Canvas, a Belgrade outfit that says it has worked with activists in more than 50 countries. Blueprint for Revolution, the handbook he wrote with Matthew Miller, is written in the register of an airport business book. But the method inside it is exact, it has been tested in more places than any academic model, and it is now free to download.
