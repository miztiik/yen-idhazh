Open source AI is likely further behind closed-source than current benchmarks indicate. If this gap grows, which is certainly possible, it would limit the usefulness of open source AI across a broad spectrum of capabilities that will be important for LMICs.
Note: If you’re new to thinking about AI, check out our primer on what open source actually means here.
Always catching up? Media cycles have framed open- and closed-source AI as a horse race
Every few months, there’s a ‘moment’ when open source AI (OSAI) seems to be ‘catching-up’ to the frontier, and this triggers widespread speculation about the comparative advantages of open- and closed-sourced models. The most memorable in the past two years may be the release of Deepseek R1, which demonstrated stunning reasoning capabilities comparable to the leading (closed) model at the time, OpenAI’s o1.
In July 2026, we had another burst of excitement from Moonshot AI’s Kimi K3. At 2.8 trillion parameters, the open-weight release is 75% larger than the previous largest open release in Deepseek’s V4 Pro, and the scaled size as well as training refinements have yielded a model with comparable capabilities to many closed frontier models – ranking #3 in Artificial Analysis at the time.
Similar to the Deepseek ‘moment,’ this has created anxiety about non-Chinese OSAI falling behind which led to widespread support for a letter by the CEO of Nvidia, Jensen Huang, on the importance of keeping model weights open - which was later followed up by an announcement in September 2026 that Nvidia would acquire the open source repository Hugging Face.
Given all this news, it’s not surprising that many governments are betting big on the future of open source, and designing strategies explicitly around it.
Within days of R1's release in 2025, India's IT minister announced that DeepSeek would be hosted on domestic servers, with Indian user data processed locally on a new state compute facility of nearly 19,000 GPUs offered at 42% below market rates. By the time global leaders gathered in New Delhi for the AI Impact Summit in February 2026, the rhetoric supporting open weight models was shaping action: Mozilla pledged its billion-dollar reserves to open source AI, with its CTO describing the commitment as “a hard-headed investment in sovereignty, resilience, and democratic legitimacy,” set against the “digital rents paid to foreign entities” that come with typical access to proprietary models.
Think tanks are often promoting the same message. Carnegie greeted R1 as proof that world-class AI can be developed affordably and adapted for Swahili, Hausa, or Amharic; authors at CSIS commended open models to the Global South on the logic that LLMs are “expensive to build but relatively cheap to fine-tune,” pointing to nascent hubs in Kenya and Nigeria. The Gulf states were one of the first movers and invested heavily: Saudi Arabia's ALLaM, an Arabic model first post-trained on open source Llama 2 and now folded into Humain, the kingdom's $100 billion AI venture, and the UAE's open-weight Falcon models both showed that national projects could begin to build atop open foundations. Where all of these efforts have since gone is far less clear, and mapping that uncertain trajectory is where we'd like to focus on for this piece.
What if open source keeping up is a moment, rather than a trend?
There is a plausible scenario in which open source, rather than keeping up, falls too far behind to be a key pillar of a national AI strategy.
The current commentary around the latest AI model release, as exciting as they can be, are not useful for clear policy thinking – we are being trained to have the reflexes of spectators at a horserace. Breathless tracking of AI’s capabilities tells us to watch the yawning and closing gap between models in the horse race, with the distance often measured by months. This gives a false impression that fluctuations in the gap are likely to continue oscillating.
However, it may be deeply unrealistic to consider the breeds of horses in this race to be equally matched. One outcome could instead be a widening capability gap, durable for long enough to limit the utility of OSAI across a range of domains.
To argue why these dynamics should be taken seriously, we will:
- Offer a few reasons for why the current gap in the horse race could be understated and an artefact of temporary dynamics.
- The structural advantages of fast following fall away in an arms race due to technical, economic, and security dynamics.
- Make the case that across varying scenarios, including an arms race, some benefits of OSAI may still be worth capturing (covered in greater detail in a subsequent post).
These dynamics run counter to the common discourse about the utility of OSAI for LMICs, which typically assert that OSAI will be ‘good enough’ and accessible - meaning that countries can enjoy strong AI capabilities from OSAI without compromising on sovereignty to gain frontier access. The implications of these trends are serious enough that they should inform policy planning today, rather than simply hoping that fruitful futures for OSAI are the default path.
The horse race: Reasons why we think the gap is understated
Benchmarks are currently being used as the go-to measure of the race between open- and closed source AI – whether measured in months or indices. But there are reasons to believe that OSAI’s gap with the frontier may be understated.
This is likely for two main reasons:
- Benchmark performance may have overstated present capabilities.
- The source of growing capabilities has primarily been fast following rather than innovation.
What exactly do we mean by these? Well, for the first, benchmark performance overstates present capabilities in three key ways:
- Open source models have often spent far more tokens than closed-source variants to tackle the same benchmarks
Allowing the models to think for longer costs more but gives open source models an advantage in performance which is under-reported. Even though token cost is often lower for open source models, price per question was found to be higher for Kimi K3 than GPT-5.6 Sol because the open source Kimi spent far more tokens on answering questions across the measured benchmarks. Some of these benchmark-hacking practices could shift in the near future. For instance, Inkling, an open source model developed by Thinking Machines, trained in the ability to control thinking efforts which reduces cost.
- Open source models consistently fall short on private benchmarks
This shortfall compared to their performance on public benchmarks implies deliberate training, as noted by EpochAI in their methodology (May 2026). The "questions" on private benchmarks are not available for labs to train their models on, and therefore serve as less hack-able tests of a model’s capabilities. The recent Kimi K3 is an extreme example. On GPQA Diamond – public since 2023, with its questions and answers freely downloadable – K3 scores 93% against the best closed model's 95%. On FrontierMath, whose questions Epoch AI keeps secret so that no model can study for the exam, the same race is not close: 72% against 89% on the standard tiers, and 39% against 88% on the hardest problems. This pattern holds generally. Measured on public benchmarks, the open–closed lag reads as roughly four to six months; on private benchmarks this lag roughly doubles. This effect likely compounds with release taxes (outlined below) as developers publishing later have more opportunities to assess which aspects of frontier models were improved on to achieve benchmark gains.
- The adoption gap between open and closed models
Although there is a capabilities gap, it’s unclear how much this explain the high adoption gap between open- and closed-source models as reported in Demirer, Fradkin, and Tadelis (2026), or by vast price differentials depending on inference provider (up to 5x), or by just general lack of knowledge about open source AI (four years post-ChatGPT is still very early days for adoption and diffusion as per past general-purpose technologies that have taken decades – approximately 80 years for electricity).
Now, in terms of the fast following versus innovation debate, it’s clear that fast following is much easier than pushing the frontier. It’s certainly the case that many of the improvements in OSAI are the result of genuinely brilliant advancements which achieve gains at high efficiency. Deepseek’s GRPO is a classic example, and it seems Kimi’s K3 has deployed similarly novel architectures through Stable LatentMoE. However, that these headlines focusing on ‘catching-up’ are so attention-grabbing proves the rule that OSAI has still never pushed the frontier on capabilities.
There are a number of factors that give fast following the edge. First, iterating on working formulas is much easier than pure innovation. Schumpeter also observed the same dynamic in capitalist economies: the pioneer's success is a signal that draws swarms of imitators to compete the profit away. Second, developers of frontier models also incur an additional release tax through the additional safety tests and high R&D costs of exploring paths in the frontier. Third, distillation, the extraction of model components by training on another model’s outputs, is reported to have aided many laggard developers – we explore this last point using available evidence in the next section.
The arms race: We shouldn’t expect this gap to remain stable, and it could easily grow further
The size of the gap between the best open and closed models is less important than whether it is growing or shrinking – measuring in ‘months behind’ obfuscates the capability dynamic. The most careful analysis of the gap found that it is growing over time. It was smallest just after the release of DeepSeek R1 in January 2025, and has grown since. Not every indicator agrees (UK AI Security Institute empirically measured a narrowing gap on cyber, although the results from the Kimi K3 update made somewhat different claims), and any of these series are noisy over such a short period of time. After all, we are only talking about, at most, three to four years. Any claims about the trend in the gap, therefore, should rely on structure: why might the fast-following strategy that produced the small gaps of the past years become harder, and how fragile will the supply of high-capability open models turn out to be.
Three factors may prove pivotal in the supply of high-capability open models: technical dynamics for fast-following, the economics of open sourcing AI, and securitisation.
To begin, we turn to an important technical tool for fast-following – distillation. This is a means of training new models to imitate the outputs of a stronger model, so that the student model inherits capabilities without repeating the full and expensive training process. Distillation on its own is a common exercise within labs to train their own models, particularly smaller versions which can then be hosted on more edge devices common in LMICs (this is a key adaptation pathway). The specific practice of extractive distillation, or illicit distillation, is what has been conjectured to be a key factor in fast following: the practice of training on a stronger model’s outputs without the approval of the original model’s developers, in order to ride the coattails of the frontier.
The practice of extractive distillation is apparently widespread, as can be inferred from its fingerprints. A statistical comparison of raw outputs across models placed Kimi K3's responses unusually close to Fable's. A simpler experiment is just asking the model for its name: both Kimi K2 and K3 call themselves Claude at rates far beyond chance. But in spite of its recent rise in prominence within the discourse on AI, there are reasons to believe that the potential of distillation may have already peaked.
First, the benefits of distillation are likely to fall. The method works by extracting what is visible in a model’s outputs, and a growing share of frontier capabilities are invisible through outputs only. The current frontier models are built on reinforcement learning within curated task environments, such as screen-recordings of developers or specialised sandboxes, and in proprietary post-training pipelines. While distilled models can imitate what better closed models say in the final output, they cannot imitate the environment they were trained in or the interim steps taken to generate that output. Apple's study of distillation scaling laws finds that imitating a model beats training from scratch only while the imitator model's compute budget is small – spend enough, and ordinary training overtakes it. Another condition is that the imitator model has to start from a capability base close enough to the model it’s trying to distil. If the imitator model is too far behind it stops benefiting from the teacher's strength at all. Distillation will likely remain an effective tool for building good, cheap models, but its strength fails as capabilities advance to accomplish more complex tasks, where less of the work is visible.
Second, AI labs are improving their defense against distillation. One of the more valuable inputs to distilled models were the reasoning traces, which show how a model is thinking about answering your prompt. Increasingly, labs are hiding these traces. Some labs have gone further, “poisoning” their models’ outputs when they suspect that it is being used for training outside models. Google, for example, says it "continuously detects, disrupts, and mitigates model extraction activity to protect proprietary logic and specialised training data, including with real-time proactive defenses that can degrade student model performance." These defenses are increasingly being layered on commercial and legal barriers, such as rate limits and contract terms. While none of these are decisive, there appears to be a defensive bias against distillation attacks.
Third, while distillation has been one of the most reliable tools for fast-following open models, it is not the only one. Large gains were made in 2024 by improvements in training techniques, such as DeepSeek’s reinforcement-learning innovations before R1 which we highlighted in the previous section. Such algorithmic improvements enable smaller companies to keep up with larger companies even as the cost of compute for a frontier model doubles every eight months. Eventually, however, capital constraints on these smaller companies will make it more and more difficult to keep up and innovate. Staying within touching distance of the frontier will increasingly involve paying escalating costs.
Partly because of this, there are very few companies releasing open models, and they are concentrated in China. The supply of frontier open models today comes entirely from a few Chinese firms – DeepSeek, Moonshot, Alibaba, Z.ai – after Meta, the standard bearer for open weights, retreated. These companies release open models as part of a market-access strategy, seeking to gain global distribution, developer usage, and leverage against the American incumbents. This strategy may change at any time, and the economic conditions of Chinese AI labs could soon force a pivot away from open-weight releases: one analysis tabulated American AI firms raising 24x more equity funding than their Chinese lab rivals, and the gap in annualised rate of revenue for 2025 was even higher at over 50x.
Beyond economics, there are also signs of greater securitisation. In June 2026, the US government ordered Anthropic to suspend all foreign access to its newest model on national-security grounds. Although these restrictions were later lifted, it’s evidence that Washington is beginning to treat frontier models as securitisable assets. Soon after, Reuters reported that China's Ministry of Commerce had held talks with Alibaba, ByteDance and Z.ai about restricting overseas access to their most advanced models, including open-weight releases and as yet unreleased models. This fits with a tiered logic already circulating in Chinese legal debate: basic tools released freely, frontier capability kept at home. Admittedly, this is hard to square with recent public announcements by Xi Jinping that have placed OSAI at the centre of China’s global AI strategy – these are uncertain times.
Another pressure that might constrain open source is on the safety side. Open weight models are far easier to misuse as their safeguards are more vulnerable to jailbreaks or fine-tuning, enabling bad actors to commit cyberattacks or assist in terrorist activity – misuse categories that would be monitored under API-based access. As OSAI increases in capability alongside the frontier, the capacity for harm through misuse goes up asymmetrically to the frontier given the lack of monitoring. As a result, proposals for pre-release capability review would bind open models far more tightly than closed ones, since a released weight file cannot be recalled. If implemented, this would create a release tax for open models which had historically enjoyed the opposite advantage to closed source models, and it was reported on August 13th that the US government would require such higher evaluations for more capable OSAI releases.
Finally, even if the gap between open and closed models does not increase, its effects will become more pronounced. The gap is conventionally quoted in months: how long ago the frontier matched what open models can just do today. As the speed of capabilities improvements increases, an unchanged lag of four to eight months between closed and open models represents a growing gap in capabilities, with no deterioration in the ability of fast-followers. If frontier AI companies succeed in speeding up their own research even more, through internal recursive self-improvement, the gap will increase even faster, while even less of it can be poached through distillation.
Many of the mechanisms above are uncertain. New distillation techniques may be discovered, governments may choose to support rather than oppose open models, scaling laws may slow down, or the Nvidia and Hugging Face consortium could herald a new era of OSAI. However, it is clear that the supply of near frontier-level open models is dependent on the continued strategic patience of a handful of firms in a single jurisdiction, whose government is actively debating whether that patience serves the national interest. This single point of failure may be sufficient to foreclose the hopes of many open model advocates for indefinite access to near-frontier capabilities that can power intelligence infrastructure. With such vulnerability, arguably the worst course of action for LMICs would be to pin all of their hopes on the high-risk prospects of OSAI remaining accessible and competitive on capabilities.
Facing these uncertainties, how can LMICs prepare for the future
Given the variety of possible futures on offer, it is tempting to conclude that no policy chosen today can be optimal across all of them. Perhaps the technical, economic, and security dynamics we have described will combine to sideline OSAI entirely. Or maybe a new distillation technique, or a change of heart in Beijing or Washington, will restore the fast-followers to touching distance. Our projection seems to introduce more uncertainties than we started with.
But there is another gap which overshadows this uncertainty for the future: the capability gap is measured in months, yet the policy response is measured in years. Everything an LMIC would need in any of these futures, whether its compute procurement, data infrastructure, technical teams capable of post-training and auditing a model, or the regulatory capacity to govern one, takes years to build and cannot be improvised on the day a given future arrives. A government that waits for the race to resolve will discover that the hard part is the capacity to act on that future.
The sound strategy is to identify no-regret investments: policies that pay off whether the gap widens, narrows, or holds. Even as the horse race keeps producing its 'moments', and the commentary keeps calling the gap.
