New research using hundreds of administrative reforms worldwide shows that cities that become regional capitals grow faster, especially where political status and market access reinforce one another.
Over the last few decades, many countries have created new states, provinces, or regions as part of broader decentralisation reforms. The World Bank's 1999/2000 World Development Report described this shift towards 'localisation' and greater power for subnational governments as one of the defining forces shaping development in the 21st century (World Bank 2000). These reforms are usually intended to improve public service delivery, bring government closer to citizens, or accommodate demands for greater local autonomy. But their effects may extend well beyond these immediate objectives. Whenever a new administrative unit is created, a city must be selected as its capital.
Becoming a regional capital is more than a change of title. Government departments and public employees move in, public investment may follow, and the city becomes the administrative centre of the new jurisdiction. These changes may also attract private investment and migrants. While the consequences of decentralisation for governance and public service delivery have been studied extensively (Narasimhan and Weaver 2024, Dahis and Szerman 2026), we know much less about whether the creation of new administrative units, and hence new regional capitals, changes where economic activity takes place.
This question is not straightforward. Firms value access to workers, suppliers, and customers, while workers value access to jobs and services (Krugman 1991, Combes et al. 2010). Political decisions may therefore have limited effects when they push activity towards places with little market potential. A new capital in a well-connected location may develop into a larger regional hub. In a more isolated location, the same change in political status may produce government offices without attracting much private activity.
A global experiment in political geography
In new research, we use hundreds of administrative reforms around the world as a global quasi-experiment in political geography (Bluhm et al. 2026). We examine how gaining regional capital status affects the growth of cities and their surroundings. First-order regions are the highest tier of administrative division below the national level, such as states, provinces, or regions. We document changes in these regions and their capitals between 1987 and 2018 (Figure 1), and combine our new global dataset on these reforms with spatial data on city boundaries, population, and night-time lights, a widely used proxy for local economic activity.

Our main comparison exploits administrative splits. When an existing region is divided, we compare the city selected as the capital of the new region with other cities that belonged to the same region before the reform. These cities share the same pre-reform regional setting and are exposed to the same decision to divide the region, but only one gains capital status. Future capitals and comparison cities follow similar growth trajectories before the reform, and the results are similar across several definitions of the comparison group. Capital status raises economic activity within and beyond the capital
Cities that become regional capitals experience greater growth in economic activity than comparable cities. Five or more years after the reform, their night-time light intensity is roughly 14–22% higher in our preferred estimates. The effect is gradual rather than immediate. It becomes visible after about two years and continues to increase thereafter, consistent with administrative relocation, construction, investment, and migration taking time. New capitals also expand physically, as measured by the size of their urban footprint.

The increase in economic activity is not confined to the capital. Nearby cities also grow faster following the reform, suggesting that the new capital does more than simply draw activity away from its immediate urban neighbours. Our data cannot, however, identify all possible losses, particularly in rural areas and very small towns that are not well captured in our city data. Economic geography matters
The average effect hides substantial differences across cities and reforms. Capital status produces larger gains when the new jurisdiction is larger, the urban system is still taking shape, and the capital has stronger economic fundamentals. Internal market access is particularly important. A city surrounded by sizeable and relatively nearby markets offers firms better access to customers, workers, and suppliers. Cities with good internal market access gain more from becoming capitals, whereas the gains are smaller in locations whose main advantage is agricultural suitability.
Political status therefore appears to complement rather than substitute for economic fundamentals. Its effects are strongest when politics and market access reinforce one another.
Why do new capitals grow more in well-connected places?
Two mechanisms help explain the growth of new capitals.
First, new capitals attract more educated migrants. Within cities that become capitals, migrants arriving after the reform tend to be more highly educated than those who arrived earlier.
Second, the broader evidence is consistent with greater public and private investment, in line with evidence that public employment can generate wider local economic effects (Becker et al. 2021). Publicly supported projects are more strongly concentrated in capital cities, particularly in education, health, water and sanitation, government, and infrastructure. Foreign direct investment is also substantially higher in capitals, especially in manufacturing, finance, insurance, and other productive activities.
This distinction is central to the policy message. Public investment responds to political status, but private investment responds especially strongly when political status is combined with favourable economic fundamentals. Firms do not follow administrative status mechanically. Their response is strongest when capital status reinforces the economic advantages of the location.
Implications for place-based policy
Our findings speak to a broader debate about whether place-based policies can shift economic activity and when local gains reflect agglomeration rather than displacement (Glaeser and Gottlieb 2008, Kline and Moretti 2014).
First and foremost, our results show that political decisions can influence where urban growth takes place. Becoming a regional capital does more than change a city's administrative title. It concentrates government activity and public investment in one location and can make that city a focal point for migrants and private investors. In urban systems that are still taking shape, political decisions may therefore help determine which locations emerge as regional hubs.
A second implication is that the choice of location matters. If one objective is to create a viable regional growth hub, governments should consider whether a candidate city has sufficient access to population, firms, and transport networks. The size of the jurisdiction matters as well, as capitals of very small regions may lack the scale needed to attract substantial private activity. Governments choose capitals for many reasons other than economic growth, but our results show that these choices nevertheless have economic consequences.
Finally, our findings do not imply that governments should only support places that already have favourable conditions. Investment in remote or disadvantaged regions may be justified on equity grounds or for reasons of political inclusion. But political designation alone is unlikely to overcome weak connectivity or limited market size.
Our evidence also does not establish whether administrative reforms raise national productivity or welfare. We document where activity grows. Positive spillovers to nearby cities are encouraging, but some activity may be drawn from rural areas, very small towns, or more distant locations. The broader lesson is that political status can redirect urban growth, but not independently of economic geography.
