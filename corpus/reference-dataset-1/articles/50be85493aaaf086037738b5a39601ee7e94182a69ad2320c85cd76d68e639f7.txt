When Iran began stopping tankers in the Strait of Hormuz during this year's war with the United States, it let some vessels through and held others back. It never closed the strait outright, and it could not have: shutting Hormuz would have strangled Iran's own oil exports and invited a response Tehran could not survive. The harassment was calibrated, and for all the alarm it caused, it was absorbed.
However, it was still enough to revive an old conversation in Delhi and Washington about chokepoints as weapons. And in that conversation, from the Indian side, one chokepoint has always stood above the rest, the Strait of Malacca, the 800-km funnel between the Andaman Sea and the South China Sea through which the bulk of China's seaborne oil passes, within reach of Indian airfields and submarines.
The idea has a long pedigree in Indian strategic writing. In a crisis over the Himalayas or in the Indian Ocean, the argument runs, India could interdict Malacca, choke Beijing's energy lifeline, and force it to relent. The idea has been that the threat of a blockade could serve as a credible instrument of military and economic deterrence against China, stronger adversary. It is an attractive idea for a country that knows it is outmatched in conventional military capabilities on land and is looking for asymmetric leverage at sea.
It also rests on a picture of Chinese energy dependence that is at least a decade out of date. The China that could have been brought to its knees by a Malacca blockade was the China of the mid-2000s. At the time, Beijing was importing oil from a handful of Gulf and African suppliers, with almost no overland alternatives, thin reserves, and an economy whose growth ran directly on imported crude.
However, over the last decade and a half, through a deliberate, sustained diversification of where its energy comes from and how it arrives, and a rapid build-out of domestic supply, China has drained most of the coercive value out of a Malacca blockade. Malacca remains a serious vulnerability, but it can no longer serve the purpose that it once could.
Around the chokepoint
China was importing about 11.1 million barrels of crude oil a day (mb/d) as of 2024, the world's largest buyer by a wide margin, and that covers roughly three-quarters of all the oil it consumes. This is the fact the vulnerability thesis rests on, and it is a genuinely large one. China leans on imported oil more heavily now than it did twenty years ago, when imports met around 40-45 per cent of its needs.
The paradox at the centre of this piece is that China's dependence on imported oil has risen even as its vulnerability to any single chokepoint has fallen. Both things are true, and they are true for the same reason. The barrels are now spread across far more sellers, and they arrive by far more routes.
In the mid-2000s, China's crude came overwhelmingly from the Persian Gulf and a few African producers like Angola and Sudan, and almost all of it came by sea, through Malacca. Today the supplier list runs to around 55 countries for oil and gas taken together, and the routes include three major overland pipelines that did not exist then.
These include the Kazakhstan-China crude line, which has been operational since 2006, the East Siberia-Pacific Ocean (ESPO) spur from Russia (from 2011), and the China-Myanmar oil and gas pipelines (from 2017), which land Middle Eastern and African crude on Myanmar's coast and pump it into Yunnan, bypassing Malacca entirely.
The current supplier mix tells the same story.
The point, buried in that last column, is that "seaborne" and "through Malacca" are no longer the same thing. Russian ESPO crude loaded at Kozmino on the Pacific reaches Chinese ports without going near the strait. Brazilian and Angolan cargoes come across the Pacific and the southern Indian Ocean by routes a blockade of Malacca does not touch. Roughly 90 per cent of China's crude still arrives by sea, but the Malacca-specific share sits meaningfully below that, and the overland pipelines carry about a tenth of the total off the water altogether.
Oil in the Bank
Diversifying supply reduces the odds of a cut-off. Storage reduces the damage if one comes anyway, by giving Beijing time to reroute, negotiate and wait a blockade out. Here China has built the largest emergency oil stockpile in the world.
The strategic and commercial stocks together give China on the order of 120 days of import cover, comfortably above the 90-day standard the International Energy Agency sets for its members.
Beijing built this deliberately, over two decades. It approved a national reserve in 2003 and broke ground in 2004 on four tank farms along the eastern seaboard, each sited next to the ports and refineries that would draw on it. Those first bases, about 100 million barrels of capacity, were filled through 2008 and 2009, and the timing was a windfall. As the financial crisis dragged crude from around $150 a barrel to $50, China poured cheap oil into its new tanks at an average cost of roughly $56 a barrel.
Later phases moved inland, to places like Lanzhou, Dushanzi, Tianjin, spreading the reserve away from a vulnerable coast. Through 2025, it poured surplus crude into tanks at over a million barrels a day, taking advantage of soft prices.
India has also built a strategic petroleum reserve, but on a much smaller scale. Its reserves are held in underground rock caverns at three sites: Visakhapatnam on the east coast, and Mangaluru and Padur on the west. Together, they can hold 5.33 million tonnes, or about 39 million barrels, and are operated by Indian Strategic Petroleum Reserves Limited under the petroleum ministry.
Those caverns hold roughly 9.5 days of India’s crude consumption. When commercial stocks held by refiners are included, national cover rises to about 74 days. That is still below the 90-day benchmark set by the IEA, which India has committed to meet as an associate member. India also took advantage of the 2020 price crash to fill its caverns cheaply, much as China had done in 2008.
It has since leased part of the Mangaluru cavern to Abu Dhabi’s ADNOC, helping offset the cost of maintaining the facility. China has used a similar commercialisation model. A second phase, cleared in 2021, would add storage at Chandikhol in Odisha and expand capacity at Padur.
The US EIA says that, for its China estimate, it counts both government-held and commercial inventories as “strategic oil inventories.” The reason is that Chinese national oil companies have reportedly been directed to maintain emergency oil in commercial stockpiles, which can effectively serve as an additional strategic reserve.
A reserve of this size does not make China immune, but it changes the math significantly. A blockade that must be sustained for four months before it begins to bite is a very different military and diplomatic proposition from one that bites in three weeks, and it hands Beijing the time to bring its overland routes, its diplomatic weight and its own countermeasures to bear.
The ballast a blockade cannot reach
A major reason why a Malacca blockade may just squeeze rather than strangle is that the fuel doing most of the work in the Chinese economy never crosses an ocean at all. It is dug up at home. Despite two decades of climate negotiations and its own carbon-neutrality pledge for 2060, China has held on to coal, and in the years when energy security and economic growth have pulled against decarbonisation, security and growth have won.
China produced about 4.78 billion tonnes of coal in 2024, more than half the world's output and more than the next ten countries combined, and it imports only around 7 per cent of what it burns. Two numbers here are often run together and should be kept apart. China's geological coal reserves are vast. Coal is more than 90 per cent of the country's fossil-fuel reserve base, enough for generations.
Separately, the coal sitting in inventory at power stations as a working buffer runs to roughly 200 million tonnes, about a month of burn, replenished continuously by a domestic rail and waterway network built to move coal from the mining provinces to the coast. That stockpile is the cushion that will keep the lights on and the factories running while it waits out a squeeze on imported oil. And in 2024 China broke ground on around 94.5 GW of new coal-fired power, the most in a decade and almost all of the world's new coal construction. None of it can be interdicted at sea.
Rising share of renewables
China is installing renewable power faster than any other country in the world, and it has sustained that pace long enough to start reshaping its entire energy system. The shift that matters most for a blockade is happening on the country's roads, where a rapid switch to electric vehicles has begun to eat into demand for oil itself.
In the single year of 2024, China added 277 GW of solar and 79 GW of wind. The solar figure alone was more than half of everything installed on the planet that year, and it pushed China past its own 2030 renewables target six years early. Non-fossil sources now generate close to 40 per cent of Chinese electricity. The Chinese Academy of Social Sciences reckons that the share of the country's total energy consumption met by clean sources, a broad official category that counts hydro, nuclear, wind, solar and natural gas, climbed from under 17 per cent in 2014 to over 31 per cent in 2024.
Moscow can't afford to say no
Diversification only helps if the diverse suppliers stay open in a crisis, and China's largest single supplier is the one least able to walk away. After 2022, Western sanctions closed Europe to Russian crude, and China, along with India, took the discounted barrels that had nowhere else to go. Russia overtook Saudi Arabia to become China's top crude supplier, at a record 108.5 million tonnes in 2024. Energy in the form of crude, gas and coal now makes up around two-thirds of everything Russia sells to China, and those sales are a load-bearing pillar of the Russian war budget.
That flips the leverage a blockade planner might assume. China buys Russian pipeline gas at roughly a third below the price European customers used to pay, and takes Russian crude at a discount to comparable grades. On the proposed Power of Siberia 2 gas line, Beijing has held out for lower prices and pushed the investment burden onto Moscow, because it has the alternatives and Russia increasingly does not. In a confrontation with India, Russia is the last supplier that would cut China off, for the plain reason that its own revenue depends on the pipes staying full.
A growing slice of Chinese oil and gas now arrives overland from a producer with no interest in joining a blockade.
Less pain per barrel
There is a final and less obvious shift, and it is worth stating carefully, because it changes the arithmetic of any blockade. Between 2014 and 2024, China's economy grew faster than its consumption of energy, so that output roughly doubled over the decade while energy use rose far less. The energy intensity of the Chinese economy, meaning the energy needed to produce each unit of GDP, has fallen steadily, by more than 70 per cent since 1990 on a longer measure and by several per cent in the last few years alone. In 2024 the economy expanded by 5 per cent while primary energy consumption rose 4.3 per cent.
The consequence for a blockade follows directly from this. When imported oil was a larger share of the fuel behind each yuan of Chinese output, cutting that oil off subtracted more from the economy than it would today. As Chinese growth has shifted from energy-hungry construction and heavy industry towards services and higher-end manufacturing, and as the power system has come to lean more on domestic coal, hydro, wind, solar and nuclear, the same barrels withheld at Malacca now buy less economic damage than they would have a decade ago. And the trend is unlike to reverse.
Cost, not capitulation
None of this makes China invulnerable. A determined interdiction across the Bay of Bengal and the northern Indian Ocean would push Chinese crude onto longer and costlier routes, draw down those reserves, and squeeze the particular grades and petrochemical chains that depend on seaborne oil, imposing genuine economic and political cost on Beijing in a prolonged conflict. But what a blockade would not do is force China to relent, inside any window that India could realistically hold it, and the practical obstacles only compound the strategic ones.
For India, that turns the strategic question from whether it can strangle China into how much pressure it can bring to bear. The leverage at sea has not disappeared so much as changed character. Malacca has become an instrument for imposing cost, complicating Chinese logistics and shaping Beijing's calculations in a drawn-out contest, a card still worth holding and worth strengthening through the Andaman and Nicobar Command, through sea-denial capability across the wider Indian Ocean, and through access arrangements with partners along the rim.
What it has ceased to be is the economic knockout blow that could bring China to heel, and Indian strategy would do better to plan for the cost it can still impose than to keep promising itself the concessions it can no longer compel.
