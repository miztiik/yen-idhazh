China's growth since 1978 came from a deliberate strategy of gradualist, experimental reform rather than shock therapy – first freeing rural labour and building light manufacturing, then shifting to investment- and credit-driven growth after 1997 – and that same investment-led model is now the source of China's property slump, high debt, and weak consumption.

China's economic transformation since 1978 is one of the most remarkable growth stories in modern history, but the methods behind it are often misunderstood. A new review by Kaiji Chen and Tao Zha traces how China's gradualist reform strategy – testing policies locally before scaling them nationally – shaped five decades of growth, and why the same strategy that lifted hundreds of millions out of poverty has now produced deep structural imbalances. On this episode of VoxDevTalks, the authors unpack the two phases of China's reform era and what other developing economies can, and cannot, learn from it.
Life under central planning before 1978
Rural households worked within collectives where land and inputs were controlled collectively and pay was tied to work points, not output. The state procured farm output at fixed plan prices, so the link between effort and income was weak. Urban workers were tied to state-owned enterprises acting as employer, landlord, and welfare state at once – the "iron rice bowl" of lifetime jobs, administratively set wages, housing, and healthcare. The hukou registration system tied access to grain rations, housing, healthcare, and education to a person's registered location, preventing labour from moving to where it would be most productive.
"You cannot freely sell the land, choose the employer, or move to a city, and the hukou system would tie access to grain rations, housing, healthcare, and education to your registered location." Kaiji Chen
Leaders could have preserved central planning, or attempted a rapid "big bang" liberalisation. They chose a third route: introducing market incentives alongside the plan, testing reforms locally, and expanding them only once they worked.
Why gradualism, not shock therapy
The strategy reflected uncertainty about how markets would function without established institutions, and concern that abrupt reform would trigger unemployment and political resistance. The household responsibility system – land stayed collectively owned but was contracted to households, who could sell output above a state quota at market prices – was piloted in Sichuan and Anhui in 1978–79 before being formalised nationwide around 1980: reform by experiment rather than decree.
Structural transformation and the rise of rural industry
The first two decades of reform mainly reallocated labour out of low-productivity agriculture. Township and village enterprises (TVEs) absorbed workers released from farming, with local governments helping them access bank credit despite lacking collateral. TVE employment grew from roughly 28 million in 1978 to 135 million by 1997, while TVE value added rose from under 6% to about 26% of GDP by 1996. One estimate attributes roughly a quarter of aggregate TFP growth between 1978 and 2010 to this labour reallocation alone.
"The growth did not come mainly from building more capital. It came mainly from using labour and existing resources much more efficiently." Kaiji Chen
Because urban hukou restrictions still barred most rural residents from relocating, industrialisation happened largely near rural communities rather than through mass migration. The formal rural–urban hukou distinction was only removed in 2014.
The shift to investment-led growth after 1997
As productivity gains from labour reallocation slowed, policy pivoted around 1997–98 towards "grasp the large, let go of the small": small and medium state enterprises were privatised or allowed to fail, while the state kept tight control of large firms in strategic sectors such as infrastructure, energy, and real estate, and growth increasingly came from capital deepening rather than productivity gains. WTO accession in 2001 locked in trade relations with the US and shifted exports towards electronics and technology-intensive goods. Housing became a market commodity too, and by 2013 property accounted for roughly 80% of urban household wealth.
Directed credit and its distortions
Credit was steered by the state through controlled banks, quantitative targets, government guarantees, and subsidised mortgage lending – first supporting collateral-poor TVEs and private firms, later favouring heavy industry, infrastructure, real estate, and large SOEs. Capital deepening accounted for more than 60% of the rise in GDP per capita during this period. But the same mechanisms reduced the labour share of income, crowded out less-connected private firms, encouraged overcapacity, and built up shadow banking and local government debt.
"Direct credit was a helping hand in one phase and accumulating distortion in another." Kaiji Chen
Consumption, debt, and the property slump
Weak household consumption remains one of China's most persistent imbalances. GDP grew around 10% while household labour income grew only about 6%, and incomplete pension and health insurance encouraged high precautionary saving. Housing amplified this – high down payments suppressed spending during the boom, and falling prices created a negative wealth effect. By July 2026, real estate investment had fallen 19% year-on-year and prices were down 20% from their 2021 peak, while total social financing stood at around 315% of GDP in 2025 – a burden made worse because local governments, which long relied on land sales for revenue.
The next phase, the authors argue, needs a genuine change of direction: shifting fiscal resources from infrastructure towards pensions, healthcare, and migration services, restructuring viable property developers, and restoring competitive neutrality between private and state firms. Fast-growing sectors like EVs, robotics, and AI will need to convert into household income and consumption demand.
Lessons for other developing economies
The transferable lesson is not China's institutional package but its underlying logic: diagnose the binding constraint at each stage of development, use experimentation where institutions are weak, and build social insurance and bank supervision early, before overinvestment sets in. What is specific to China – its scale of rural labour, a centralised state paired with decentralised local experimentation, and access to Hong Kong and Taiwanese capital at a favourable moment for global trade – should not be copied mechanically. Rwanda is cited as one country that has drawn heavily on China's investment-driven model with considerable success, though the same risk of unbalanced growth applies abroad as it does at home.
